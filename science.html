What is a temperature inversion?

Normally air temperature decreases with altitude in the lower atmosphere. A thermal inversion is a layer where temperature instead increases with height. A stable layer can trap moisture below it, but an inversion alone does not imply dense fog or a picturesque cloud sea.

The cloud sea you want also requires low cloud or fog around Boulder and sufficiently clear conditions near the Flatirons or Green Mountain. These are distinct physical observations and should eventually be labeled separately.

What are the two models learning?

XGBoost learns nonlinear interactions between surface humidity, cloud forecasts, wind, interpolated temperature gradients, time of day and season.

1D CNN scans standardized vertical profiles of temperature, relative humidity and horizontal wind components. Each profile spans 0–2,000 meters above local model ground, sampled on 25 levels by interpolation between available GFS pressure layers. That interpolated resolution does not add atmospheric information.

Both models use the same GFS forecast times and a chronological train/validation/test split. Independent, real observed labels are required to make the validation physically meaningful.

What is the limitation of proxy training?

Until real labels are available, the optional training command constructs a proxy outcome from the historical weather model's own low cloud, mid/high cloud and visibility predictions. This teaches post-processing of a forecast-model diagnostic, not the observed reality. It can reproduce a model's mistakes.

Historical stitched forecasts use the first hours of repeated model runs, while a 7-day live forecast has much longer lead times. A proper backtest must use archived forecasts at the same lead times paired with independent observations. A statistically calibrated proxy score cannot be advertised as a real-world inversion probability.

Cloud-top height, summit visibility and hiking safety are not estimated in live mode. Historic preset values are fictional demo scenes.
